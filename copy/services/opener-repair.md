## Common opener problems we see

Openers tend to fail in pretty recognizable ways. These are the ones we hear about most:

* **It won't respond at all.** You press the remote or the wall button and nothing happens. Could be power, a dead remote battery, the wall switch, or the logic board inside the unit.
* **The door reverses on its own.** It starts down, then backs up before it reaches the floor. Nine times out of ten that's the safety sensors, either knocked out of line or with a dirty lens. Sometimes it's the force setting or something binding on the track.
* **Grinding, rattling, or clunking.** A worn gear or sprocket, a loose chain, or a tired motor will make an opener loud. That noise is usually warning you that something's about to give.
* **The remote or keypad won't connect.** Dead batteries are the easy one. Past that, remotes can need reprogramming, or the antenna or receiver on the opener may be the trouble.
* **It runs but the door doesn't move.** The motor hums and the carriage travels, but the door stays put. That can be a stripped gear, a broken trolley, or a disconnected arm.

A few of these you can rule out yourself, like swapping a remote battery or wiping off the sensor eyes. If it's more than that, we'll sort it out for you.

## The main types of openers

If you're replacing an opener, the drive type is the big decision. Each one moves the door a little differently.

**Chain drive** uses a metal chain, sort of like a bike chain, to pull the door up. It's the workhorse, tough and affordable. The tradeoff is noise, so if your garage sits under a bedroom, you'll hear it.

**Belt drive** swaps the chain for a reinforced rubber belt. Much quieter and smoother, which is why people pick it for attached garages and rooms over the garage. It costs a bit more than a chain.

**Screw drive** turns a threaded steel rod to move the door. Fewer moving parts and not much upkeep. These sit in the middle on noise and handle our heat fine.

**Wall mount** openers, also called jackshaft openers, mount on the wall right beside the door instead of up on the ceiling. They free up the ceiling for storage or a tall garage and run nice and quiet. They're a great fit for high or cathedral style ceilings.

**Smart and WiFi openers** let you open, close, and check the door from your phone, wherever you happen to be. A lot of them run through the myQ app, and you can get an alert when you've left the door up. Many newer LiftMaster and Chamberlain units have this built in, and we can add it to plenty of existing setups too.

## Repair it or replace it?

Plenty of opener problems are a straightforward repair: a new gear, a sensor realignment, a logic board, a fresh remote. If the unit is in good shape, fixing it is the smarter, cheaper move, and that's exactly what we'll tell you.

Replacement makes more sense when:

* **The opener is around ten to fifteen years old.** That's the normal lifespan, and parts for older units get harder to find.
* **It keeps breaking.** If you're calling every few months, a new unit usually costs less over time than chasing one repair after another.
* **It has no safety sensors.** Openers built before the mid 1990s often lack the photo eyes that stop the door from closing on a kid, a pet, or the bumper of your car. That alone is worth a replacement.
* **You want features it can't do.** Quiet operation, phone control, or battery backup so the door still opens when the power's out, which comes in handy during storm season around here.

You'll get an honest call either way. If a repair buys you several more good years, we'll do the repair.

## Opener repair FAQs

**My garage door won't close and the opener light keeps blinking. What's wrong?**
That blinking light is usually the safety sensors. The two little eyes near the floor need a clear line of sight to each other. Check for a bin or a box in the way, wipe the lenses, and nudge them back into line until the lights hold steady. If it still won't close, give us a call.

**Is it worth repairing an old opener, or should I just replace it?**
Depends on the unit. If it's under ten years old and the fix is small, repair it. If it's pushing fifteen years, breaking often, or has no safety sensors, a new opener is usually the better money. We'll walk you through the honest math.

**How long should a garage door opener last?**
About ten to fifteen years for most units. How often you use it and a little routine maintenance both make a difference. Keeping the door itself balanced helps too, since the opener isn't fighting a heavy door every cycle.

**Can I control a new opener from my phone?**
Yep. Smart openers connect to WiFi and run through an app like myQ, so you can open or close the door and get alerts from anywhere. We can install a smart opener, or in a lot of cases add the feature to the one you already have.

**Do you work on my brand?**
Most likely. We service LiftMaster, Chamberlain, Genie, Craftsman, Linear, and Wayne Dalton, among others. Tell us what you've got when you call and we'll come prepared.
