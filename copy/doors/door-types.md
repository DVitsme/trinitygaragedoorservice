# Garage Door Types & Styles

Shopping for a garage door comes down to three questions. How does it open, what is it made of, and how do you want it to look. Down here there's a fourth, how well it stands up to a storm. This page walks through your options so you can show up to your estimate knowing what you want. When you're ready, our [installation](/services/installation/) and [replacement](/services/replacement/) pages cover the rest.

## How the door opens

Most people never think about this, because almost every modern home uses the same kind. Here's the rundown anyway.

**Sectional.** The standard garage door in the United States, and what we install most. It's built from horizontal panels hinged together, riding on rollers in a track. As it opens, it bends back and tucks up under the ceiling. Sectional doors take openers well and are the easiest type to build for wind and impact ratings, which matters a lot in Florida.

**Roll up.** Made of narrow slats that coil around a drum above the opening, with no track running back into the garage. You see these mostly on commercial buildings, though they work on homes too, especially where there's little ceiling room to work with.

**The older styles.** A few designs still show up on older or special homes. Side hinged doors swing out from the middle like barn doors. Slide to the side doors run along the wall on a track. Tilt up doors are a single solid slab that pivots up, either forming a small canopy out front or sliding back into the garage. These are rare in new construction now. One piece tilt up doors in particular are hard to insulate, seal, and rate for wind, which is another reason sectional doors took over down here.

## What it's made of

The material sets the look, the upkeep, and how the door handles our heat, humidity, and salt air. Here's the honest take on the four we work with.

**Steel.** The most common choice, and a good one. It's strong and affordable, and it barely needs upkeep, and it's the usual starting point for an impact rated door. You can get it smooth, raised panel, or pressed to look like wood. The one thing to know near the coast: steel can rust where it gets scratched or dinged, so go with a well coated, galvanized door and give it a rinse now and then.

**Wood.** Nothing matches real wood for warmth and custom looks, and it suits certain homes beautifully. The catch is upkeep, and Florida is hard on it. Sun, humidity, and salt swell and warp wood and fade the finish, so it needs regular sealing and refinishing. Gorgeous if you'll maintain it, frustrating if you won't. If you love the look but not the work, read the composite note below.

**Aluminum and glass.** Light, modern, and full of daylight, with slim aluminum frames around glass panels. The big advantage near the water is that aluminum doesn't rust, so it shrugs off salt air better than steel. Two things to keep in mind: aluminum dents more easily than steel, and if you're in a storm zone the glass has to be impact rated to meet code, which we cover below.

**Composite and faux wood.** The wood look without the wood headaches. Composite and fiberglass doors give you real grain and color over a core that won't rot, warp, or corrode, which is a genuine win in our climate. Over many years fiberglass can fade in the sun and get brittle, so quality matters, but for a lot of folks who want the wood look this is the smart pick.

Roughly speaking, a plain single layer steel door is the budget end, insulated steel and composite sit in the middle, and real wood and full glass aluminum doors run the highest.

## The look

Material is what the door is made of. Style is the face it shows the street.

**Raised panel** is the classic look, rows of rectangular panels, clean and easy to live with. It fits Colonial, ranch, and most traditional homes, which is to say most of the street.

**Carriage house** doors mimic the old swing open barn doors, usually with decorative hardware and a row of windows up top. These days they're almost always sectional doors styled to look the part, so you get the charm without the swing. Great on Craftsman, farmhouse, Spanish, and a lot of Florida custom homes.

**Modern** leans clean and simple, either a smooth flush face or a full view door of glass and aluminum that floods the garage with light. A natural fit for contemporary and midcentury homes, and popular for shops and indoor outdoor spaces.

## Insulation, and why it matters here

Insulation is rated by R value, and a higher number means more resistance to heat. Doors come in three builds:

* **Single layer** is one skin of steel, no insulation. Cheapest and loudest, fine for a detached garage you don't spend time in.
* **Double layer** adds a board of insulation behind the skin. A solid middle ground.
* **Triple layer** sandwiches foam between two skins. Highest R value, quietest, and the most solid and dent resistant.

In Florida, insulation earns its keep. If your garage is attached, shares a wall with the house, or has a room above it, an insulated door keeps heat out and takes a load off your AC. Foam filled doors are quieter too. One honest note: the R value on the sticker is measured at the center of the panel, so the whole installed door performs a bit lower. It's a good way to compare doors, just not a promise.

## Built for Florida storms

A garage door is usually the biggest opening on the house, and that makes it a weak point in a hurricane. If a door blows in, wind gets inside and pushes up on the roof and out on the walls, which is how a bad day turns into a roof off the house. So down here the door is doing real structural work during a storm.

A few things to know:

* **Florida code sets a wind rating for every garage door** based on where your house sits. The door has to be rated for the pressure at your address.
* **Much of Tampa Bay is a wind borne debris zone,** and not only along the coast. In a debris zone the opening also has to handle flying debris, which means either an impact rated door or a standard door with approved storm protection you put up ahead of a storm.
* **Impact rated is not the same as wind rated.** A wind rated door resists pressure. An impact rated door resists pressure and a debris strike. Insulation has nothing to do with either one.
* **Bracing kits are not a real substitute.** A brace stiffens a door against pressure, but it won't stop debris from punching through, and most come off after the storm. An impact rated door is built for both and stays in place year round. It can also earn you a wind mitigation credit on your homeowners insurance.

One thing we'll clear up: the strictest hurricane rules, the ones that require Miami Dade approvals, apply to Miami Dade and Broward counties, not Tampa Bay. ## Common questions about door types

**What's the most popular type of garage door?**
An insulated sectional steel door, by a wide margin. It's affordable and low maintenance, it takes any style, and it's easy to wind and impact rate.

**What's the best door material for Florida?**
It depends on the look you want and how close you are to the water. Insulated steel is the all around favorite. Right on the coast, aluminum and composite resist salt and humidity better. Wood is beautiful but needs the most upkeep in our climate.

**Do I really need an insulated door?**
If your garage is attached or has living space above or beside it, yes, it's worth it for comfort and your AC bill. For a standalone garage you barely use, you can skip it.

**Does my garage door have to be impact rated?**
In much of Tampa Bay, your opening has to handle wind borne debris, which usually means an impact rated door or approved storm protection. We'll tell you what your address requires before you buy.

**Steel or aluminum if I live near the water?**
Aluminum won't rust, which is a real plus by the coast, but it dents easier. A well coated steel door with good upkeep also holds up fine. We'll walk you through the trade off for your spot.
